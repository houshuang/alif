This is direct subjective evidence of variable effort in resolving written forms and accessing meaning, and of answer-supported recognition on at least one item. The 14.906-second correct volunteer response should therefore not be described as unaided retrieval. The old task cannot distinguish whether Arabic recognition preceded the options or was reconstructed using them. Word-choice accuracy remains a valid description of performance in that interface, not proof of independent immediate access.

The priority hypothesis shifts toward reducing ambiguity and processing effort during meaningful reading, with an item-dependent role for tashkeel. This does not establish a universal vowel effect, a single underlying deficit, or that sentence-level integration plays no role. Enjoyment and effort coexisted; low effort and high engagement must be measured separately. The learner's report strengthens the rationale for retaining available vowel support while exploring gradual independence on familiar forms, rather than fading it solely from canonical review history.

For the next short assessment, separate the initial Arabic reading interval from meaning-option display, then check meaning; self-declared readiness is still not pure lexical-access timing. Use fresh matched items with vowel condition and answer positions balanced independently. A small across-item comparison remains exploratory, and showing the same item twice introduces priming. Include a short coherent passage, identify actual sticking points retrospectively, provide targeted support, and reread to compare perceived effort. Rereading improvement includes familiarity and support; only later fresh material can test transfer. The aim is to choose useful reading support, not to repeat the broad accuracy test or engineer faster guessing.

## Interpretation and design limitations

The strongest inference is that, for two short vowelized narratives, meaning was recoverable before assistance while reading felt effortful. The sentence items give no evidence of widespread failure on these eight tested meanings with vocabulary supplied. They do not rule out syntax-related processing cost, unfamiliar constructions elsewhere, or difficulties integrating longer texts. The relative-clause response is a useful follow-up candidate, not a diagnosis from one item.

Changed forms appear worth investigating: all basic forms were answered correctly, both errors involved changed forms, and median correct responses were slower in the changed-form group. However, different lemmas were used between conditions, lengths differed, exposure histories differed, and a design defect tied correct-option positions closely to condition (all six vowelized changed forms used option 0, all six unvowelized basic forms option 1, all six unvowelized changed forms option 2, and five vowelized basic forms option 3). The measured difference cannot be called a pure morphology cost. Any follow-up should balance answer positions independently and match items more carefully.

Reported medians by vowel condition (basic 3.464/4.265 s with/without vowels; changed 6.299/7.898 s) are descriptive only. This design does not justify changing global vowel policy. All passages and sentence probes were vowelized; ordinary unvowelized literary reading remains untested.

The earlier optional explanation of the “still” construction was not opened. Nevertheless that construction had appeared in the preceding passage, so its sentence success is not a wholly fresh encounter. Help on the second passage consisted only of word meanings after accurate first answers; no causal benefit can be assigned to that help.

No interruption flags does not establish clean attention. Word times include reading the English choices and tapping. Correct multiple-choice answers can sometimes be obtained through elimination. No normative speed cutoffs, CEFR estimate, general vocabulary-size estimate, or diagnosis is warranted.

Energy reported as Plenty throughout this session is consistent with the user's warning not to assume permanently low capacity; it does not show that assessment design caused engagement or that similar time is routinely available. The later process account is recorded above. A direct comparison with Momo and the device used remain unspecified.

## Proposed next step

Continue the small reading bridge rather than launch a general grammar course or increase vocabulary intake based on this battery. Use one coherent, interesting strand, in roughly 40–60-word portions initially (a trial choice grounded in what was just manageable). Fit reading into existing Arabic time. Read for meaning, use immediate help for actual blockers, then reread once with attention to the unfolding meaning. Continue to the next portion when this feels possible, rather than imposing repeated rehearsal to a time target.

Collect a few exact sticking points from real reading. Where the lemma is familiar, explain the displayed form and revisit it in a fresh sentence. Reserve construction practice for demonstrated bottlenecks; the relative clause is one candidate. Do not convert every slow recognition into a failed scheduled review.

After several short sessions, compare a fresh passage of similar length/difficulty/support with this baseline. Look jointly at comprehension, effort, lookups, and willingness to continue. Improvement only on a memorized passage is not sufficient evidence of transfer. Repeated-reading EFL studies give reasons to try this, but transfer findings vary and do not establish this learner's response in Arabic: [Chang & Millett, 2013](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/276); [Taguchi & Gorsuch, 2002](https://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/April2002/taguchi/taguchi.pdf).

A later short diagnostic, if needed, should distinguish reading a known base from recognizing a changed form, with balanced response positions and a fresh delayed transfer item. A brief account of how the learner handled a hard sentence may be more informative immediately than another large test.
