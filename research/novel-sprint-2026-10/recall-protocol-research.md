# Weekly L1 oral-recall reading test: research summary and protocol

Researched 2026-10-04 for a personal Arabic literature-reading project. Weekly test: fresh 150–200 word unvocalized novel passage, timed silent reading, no lookups, then oral free recall in L1 (English/Norwegian), recorded and transcribed, scored against a pre-written idea-unit list; then 2–3 probe questions; words per minute recorded. Statements marked **[inference]** are my own conclusions, not taken from a source.

## 1. The L1 recall protocol (Bernhardt 1991 and after)

**Standard procedure.** The learner reads an L2 passage silently; the text is then removed; the learner immediately produces everything they remember, in their L1. Bernhardt (1991) called the immediate written recall "the purest measure of L2 reading comprehension", and most studies have subjects recall in their native language so that L2 production ability does not confound the comprehension score ([Heinz 2004, RFL](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/6ca8df3f-f47f-4684-b801-6c15d0ae4985/content); [Brantmeier, Strube & Yu 2014, RFL](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1031310.pdf)). Lee (1986) showed that learners recall significantly more idea units when recalling in L1 than in L2 ([MLJ](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1986.tb05287.x)). Khaldieh (2001) used exactly this design for Arabic: an unvowelled expository text, then immediate written recall in English, for 32 American learners over three years; vocabulary knowledge correlated strongly with recall, knowledge of iʿrāb did not ([ERIC EJ635162](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ635162)).

**Immediacy and hidden text.** Recall is always immediate, and the text disappears before recall begins (Heinz 2004: "once they indicated that they were ready to write their recalls, the text disappeared"). Alderson and Lee object that immediate recall is partly a memory test; Heinz notes the objection is minimised precisely because recall is immediate. Subjects in Heinz's study were allowed to read "as many times as desired" with unlimited time; your single timed read is a stricter variant and is what you need for a wpm measure **[inference]**.

**Passage length.** Published L2 recall studies use about 200 words (Heinz 2004: three authentic German texts, ~200 words each), 200–250 words (French studies cited in [Brantmeier 2006](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ759836.pdf)), up to 525–646 words (Brantmeier et al. 2014). Your 150–200 words sits at the short end of the norm, which is appropriate for a weekly test **[inference]**.

**Instruction wording.** The standard written instruction is "Please write down as much as you can remember about what you just read" (Brantmeier et al. 2014). Riley & Lee (1996) showed task wording changes content: subjects told to *summarize* produced significantly more main ideas, subjects told to *recall* produced more details ([Language Testing](https://doi.org/10.1177/026553229601300203)). Fix the wording and never vary it.

**How units are defined and weighted.**
- *Pausal units* (Johnson 1970): a native speaker reads the passage aloud and inserts a bracket at every natural pause; everything within a bracket is one unit. Bernhardt (1991) validated pausal units against Meyer's (1975) hierarchical content-structure analysis: the correlation between the two systems was r = .96 on one text but only .54 on a second; with additional weighted scoring it rose to .84. She concluded that both tap the same reading behaviour and that the pausal unit is far more efficient to score (Brantmeier et al. 2014).
- *Idea units* (Carrell 1983; Riley & Lee 1996): "an idea, proposition, or constituent structure"; each piece of information that adds meaning counts as one unit; a small-to-medium grain size is used, and the list is agreed among raters in advance. Idea units typically outnumber pausal units about 2:1 (Brantmeier et al.: 52 idea vs 26 pausal units for a 525-word text). Idea and pausal counts correlate strongly, and the relationship does not depend on learner background.
- *Weighting*: Bernhardt's scoring templates rate each pausal unit 1–4 for importance to passage content, the weight being the mean rating given by proficient readers; Meyer's system assigns top/high/mid/low levels; Kintsch & van Dijk argued unweighted counts ignore differential semantic importance ([Aweiss 1993](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED366225.pdf)). However, both Heinz (2004) and Schmidt-Rinehart (1994) found the weighted four-point score statistically equivalent to the unweighted count. Heinz adds that L2 learners may rate propositional importance differently from native raters.
- *Partial credit and paraphrase*: standard practice credits literal recall or a paraphrase of the unit; "logical synonyms" acceptable in context are agreed by raters in advance (Heinz 2004, e.g. "Soviet leader" for Gorbachev).

**Reliability.** Inter-rater reliability for presence/absence scoring is high: .97 (pausal) and .96 (idea) in Brantmeier et al. 2014; .98 across raters and texts in Heinz 2004; intraclass correlation .98 for idea-unit retell scoring with 82–95% item agreement in [Reed & Petscher 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4563821). Bernhardt (1991) notes hand scoring takes up to 10 minutes per recall.

**Oral versus written recall.** Kellogg (2007) found spoken recall of text produced more correctly recalled propositions but also more *major distortions* (e.g. inferences) than written recall ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17892086/)). Sletova (2023), with 22 intermediate-low L2 Russian learners, found written recall more accurate than spoken; she scored correct propositions, distorted propositions and reformulations separately ([Foreign Language Annals](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/flan.12642)). Horiba (1996) found that L1 retrieval facilitates more elaboration and inference than L2 retrieval ([SSLA, cited here](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1157548.pdf)). **[inference]** Oral L1 recall therefore maximises retrieved content but inflates distortions and elaborations, so distortions must be coded explicitly rather than folded into "not recalled".

## 2. Prompted / cued recall without leaking content

No validated two-phase "free recall, then neutral cued recall, scored separately" protocol exists in the L2 reading-recall literature that I could find. The following combines three adjacent evidence bases.

**Retell research in reading assessment.** [Reed & Petscher 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4563821) (Grades 6–8, N = 527, text concealed immediately after a one-minute oral read) compared four conditions. "Tell me everything you remember reading in the passage" beat "Tell me in your own words what this passage was mostly about" with d = .62; adding a single neutral follow-up, "Do you remember anything else?", added d = .40; the two changes combined gave d = .96; a silent-rereading opportunity added nothing (d = .03). Their recommendation: use a "tell everything" prompt, include one follow-up encouragement, score by percentage of predetermined idea units with acceptable alternative wordings.

**Reading inventories.** The Qualitative Reading Inventory removes the text, asks for an unaided retell "as though telling it to someone who never heard it before", then asks once whether there is anything else to add, then asks comprehension questions; retelling and questions are always scored separately, and retelling always precedes questions ([QRI-5 chapter](https://www.pearsonhighered.com/assets/samplechapter/0/1/3/7/0137019238.pdf)). The Narrative Scoring Scheme likewise limits examiner prompts to "encouragement to begin the story and open-ended cues to continue" ([Heilmann et al. 2010](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2009/08-0024))).

**Forensic interviewing (NICHD Investigative Interview Protocol).** This is the strongest evidence on how prompt type biases recall. The protocol's utterance typology ([Lamb et al. 2007, review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2180422/)): *facilitators* ("ok", "uh-huh"); *invitations* ("Tell me everything that happened"); *cued invitations* that re-use only what the interviewee has already said ("You mentioned X. Tell me more about that", "Then what happened?"); *directive* wh-questions about content already disclosed ("What colour was that shirt?"); *option-posing* and *suggestive* prompts. Accuracy declines linearly from open invitations to cued invitations to directive to option-posing questions ([analogue study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24341318)), and open prompts yield three to five times more detail per prompt. A cued invitation built only from the learner's own words leaks nothing; a wh-question about something they did not mention leaks content.

**Order of free and cued recall.** A study varying the order found children's story recall was more coherent in immediate free recall than in recall after questioning ([ERIC ED222590](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED222590)). Free recall must come first; content-bearing probes come last.

**Cued vs free recall of text generally.** Nevid et al. (2016) found no difference in retention between cued (study-question) and uncued recall of a textbook passage ([IJ-SoTL](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1134763.pdf)); in vocabulary assessment, cued and free recall also gave equivalent scores ([Academia](https://www.academia.edu/33909604/Cued_vs_free_recall_for_assessing_L2_vocabulary_acquisition)). **[inference]** For a single learner tracked over time, the free/prompted split is worth keeping because the free score is the purer index and the prompted increment tells you how much is "in there" but not spontaneously retrieved.

## 3. Coding intrusions and distortions

**Standard categories.** Narrative-recall research codes each response as *veridical/verbatim*, *gist* (paraphrase, correct meaning), or *distortion* (incorrect meaning), and codes intrusions as *additions* (acts or details not in the story) and *transformations* ([script-recall studies](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232591682_Effect_of_script_structure_on_children's_story_recall)). Horiba (1996) codes L2 readers' recalls into text propositions plus three inference types: within-text inferences (logical interpretations supported by the text), elaborative inferences (text plus reader knowledge), and incorrect inferences ([cited in RFL](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1157548.pdf)). Sletova (2023) uses correct propositions / distorted propositions / reformulations. Kellogg (2007) distinguishes major from minor distortions, with inferences counted as major.

**Practical scoring rules.** The TALA idea-unit guide: a unit needs a subject and a predicate, one-word answers get no credit; synonyms and reasonable approximations are accepted; "minor inaccuracies that do not alter the basic idea" (e.g. a mispronounced proper noun) are credited; when a specific factual detail such as a year is part of the unit it must be included for credit ([TALA handout](https://d1yqpar94jqbqm.cloudfront.net/documents/U4M2Handout6.pdf)). Wechsler Logical Memory uses parallel verbatim and gist scoring systems with high inter-rater reliability ([JCEN](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01688638708405063)).

**Bernhardt's six factors.** These are a *diagnostic* layer for explaining errors, not a scoring scheme. From Bernhardt (1990, 1991) as quoted in Heinz 2004: text-based components are (1) word recognition (attachment of a semantic value), (2) phonemic/graphemic decoding (recognising words by sound or visual match), (3) syntactic feature recognition (relationships between words); extra-text components are (4) intratextual perception (reconciling each part of the text with what precedes and follows), (5) prior knowledge (whether the discourse is sensible given the reader's world knowledge), (6) metacognition (the extent to which the reader thinks about what they are reading). Bernhardt's developmental claim: "errors in understanding can reveal development in literacy", and the mix of error types shifts as proficiency increases. Aweiss (1993) illustrates with beginner Arabic-L2 recalls: "watched a film once a week at the Radio City theater in Cairo" recalled as "listen to a programme from Radio City" (word-recognition failure plus prior-knowledge intrusion); "Samir came from Syria and studied at Georgetown" expanded into facts about Damascus not in the text (knowledge-telling intrusion). Aweiss also proposes a four-level Constructive Activity Scale (fragmented associations / knowledge-details retelling / assimilation / problem-solving and integration) for rating the depth of each recalled proposition.

**Specific cases you asked about [inference, consistent with the sources above]:** wrong agent or patient, missed or added negation, misassigned pronoun referent, wrong temporal or causal link = *distortion* of that unit (0 credit, counted separately); invented event or detail = *intrusion* (false if contradicting the text, elaborative if plausible); correct core predicate with one required element missing = partial credit.

## 4. Reading speed norms

- **L2 reading generally.** Studies of L2 readers cluster at 100–150 wpm with groups commonly under 100; Nation's target is 250 wpm with about 70% comprehension on material where every word is known; below 100 wpm memory and concentration suffer ([RFL fluency review](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1316861.pdf); [timed-reading study](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1176224.pdf)). Proficient L1 readers: 250–300 wpm.
- **Native Arabic adults.** Reading unvocalized sentences aloud at large font: 166 correct words per minute (range 145–188), 184 standard-length (5-character) words per minute, N = 53; vocalization was deliberately omitted "as they are absent in everyday materials" ([Alabdulkader & Leat 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5383463/)). Native university students on unvocalized passages: 164 wpm in a standard font ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S188842961630005X)). A cross-language comparison reports silent Arabic at ~138 wpm versus 228 for English ([Iris Reading](https://irisreading.com/average-reading-speed-in-various-languages/), a secondary source). Native children in Gulf bilingual schools reading vocalized MSA aloud: 20 cwpm in Grade 1 to 66 cwpm in Grade 6 ([Taha Thomure et al. 2022](https://knepublishing.com/index.php/gespr/article/download/11493/18619)).
- **Arabic as a foreign language.** I found **no peer-reviewed wpm norms** for adult L2 readers of Arabic. Hansen (2010, 71 learners at three levels plus 24 natives) found that pseudoword decoding and text-reading speed improve markedly from beginner to intermediate and then *stall*, concluding the script is "a major obstacle to the establishment of automatic word recognition" ([MLJ](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2010.01094.x)). Online learner benchmarks (beginner 20–40 wpm on vocalized text, intermediate 60–100, advanced 150–200 on unvocalized text) are non-scholarly ([arabify](https://arabify.org/arabic-reading-practice)).
- **Effect of tashkeel.** For skilled native readers, fully vocalized text is read *more slowly* but with higher accuracy and comprehension (Abu-Rabia 1997–2007, summarised in [Taha 2019 review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330858787_The_Role_of_Short_Vowels_in_Reading_Arabic_A_Critical_Literature_Review)); in lexical decision, unvowelized words are recognised faster and more accurately ([Hermena et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10339-017-0830-9)); skilled adolescents report vowel signs as a hindrance. Results across studies conflict, partly due to uncontrolled measures ([Rabie-Ahmed & Mohamed, JNCOLCTL review](https://ncolctl.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/Instructed-Reading-and-Vocabulary-in-Arabic-as-a-Foreign-Language-A-Research-Agenda.pdf)). For L2 learners, Midhwah & Alhawary (2020) compared two programmes using a vowelized versus an unvowelized textbook and found an advantage in reading speed, accuracy and comprehension for learners *trained* with the vowelized textbook (reported in the same review). **[inference]** Keep the weekly test text unvocalized throughout so wpm is comparable week to week; vocalization in the test would both slow skilled reading and change what is measured.

## 5. Sensitivity of recall vs multiple choice vs sentence verification; automated scoring

- **Recall vs discrete-point tests.** "Not only is the recall protocol more sensitive than discrete-point tests, but its sensitivity becomes more pronounced as reading proficiency increases" (Brisbois 1992, quoted in Heinz 2004). Wolf (1993) showed scores vary with task type (free recall, sentence completion, multiple choice) and with the language of the task ([Foreign Language Annals](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1944-9720.1993.tb02289.x)). Brantmeier found reader gender × passage interactions on both recall and multiple choice, with gender explaining more variance in recall ([RFL 2006](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ759836.pdf)). Multiple choice is criticised for pre-determining the answers and leaving no room for inference (Brantmeier et al. 2014).
- **Sentence Verification Technique.** Pichette et al. (2014), 171 adult L2 English learners: high internal consistency, fits the Rasch model, in the recommended difficulty range, but correlates only r = .43 with self-assessed proficiency ([TQMP](https://r-libre.teluq.ca/449/)). SVT is sensitive to text readability and reading skill and is preferred when the goal is comprehension of a specific text rather than general ability ([SVT review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/288254477_The_Sentence_Verification_Technique_A_practical_procedure_teachers_can_use_to_develop_their_own_reading_and_listening_comprehension_tests)). **[inference]** Useful as a cheap cross-check; weaker than recall as the sole weekly measure.
- **Automated / LLM scoring of recall.** GPT-4o scoring of Craft Story 21 recall transcripts against standard criteria correlated r = .65 with human raters for both immediate and delayed recall, with inter-rater ICC comparable to humans (~.58), but with hallucinations and run-to-run score variability; the authors judge it premature for clinical use ([Hong et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12739032/)). Embedding-similarity scoring against LLM-segmented narrative events reached split-half ρ ≈ .62–.64 against human scores but is explicitly gist-based and does not detect intrusions or distortions ([Nature Comm. Psych. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12705437/); [language-agnostic L1 speech recall](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12125525/)). Heinz (2004) showed a rule-based synonym matcher against a pausal-unit template correlated well with hand scoring. **[inference]** Use an LLM as a *first-pass* scorer with the fixed idea-unit list and fixed rubric, require it to quote the transcript span supporting every credited unit, set temperature to 0 and run twice, and hand-check every distortion and intrusion decision.

## 6. Practice effects from weekly repetition

- Practice effects arise from "general testing factors" (learning the task) plus content retention; alternate forms attenuate but do not eliminate them, and "alternate forms of a test do not necessarily share their statistical properties" and "are often quite different in difficulty" ([Goldberg et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3574868/); [Alzheimer's trial literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4876902/)). Forms differing only in non-salient surface features show retest effects comparable to identical forms ([working-memory meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-018-1461-6)).
- Repeated free-recall testing itself trains retrieval organisation ("testing creates retrieval schemas that guide recall"; [Zaromb & Roediger 2010](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/MC.38.8.995)), and free recall of one text enhances learning of the next ([Pastötter & Bäuml](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23297100/)). Heinz (2004) gave subjects three practice recalls before data collection for exactly this reason.
- **[inference]** Expect an upward drift in recall percentage over the first 3–4 weeks that reflects task-wiseness, not reading gains; run two or three unscored practice sessions first, then treat week 1 as baseline. Passage rotation is automatic with fresh text, but difficulty is not: log lexical coverage (98% known tokens is the comprehension threshold in [Hu & Nation 2000](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstreams/be187723-ba8b-433c-9472-b3b4ac847b86/download)), passage length, sentence count and embedded clauses (Brantmeier et al. 2014's difficulty descriptors), report moving averages, and read recall together with wpm and probe accuracy rather than alone.

## Recommended protocol

1. **Passage.** 150–200 words, never seen before, unvocalized, from the same novel or same genre each week. Log word count, sentence count, and % tokens known according to Alif, so that difficulty can be controlled post hoc.
2. **Idea-unit list, prepared in advance.** Either pausal units (bracket the passage at the natural pauses of a native read-aloud, or of a careful L1 translation **[inference]**) or clause-level idea units; expect 20–35 units for 150–200 words. Tag each unit main / supporting / detail. Write 2–3 probe questions on units a reader might plausibly miss. Pre-list acceptable synonyms.
3. **Read.** Silent, timed, no lookups. Stop the timer when the learner says "done". wpm = words ÷ minutes.
4. **Hide the text** and start recording immediately.
5. **Free recall.** One fixed instruction every week (Reed & Petscher "tell everything" form), in the learner's L1, e.g. Norwegian: *"Fortell alt du husker fra teksten, i den rekkefølgen du vil. Ta deg god tid."* English: *"Tell me everything you remember from the text, in any order. Take your time."* During free recall the interviewer may only use facilitators ("mm", "ok") and silence.
6. **Neutral continuation prompts.** Maximum three, only after a pause of at least 5 seconds or an explicit "that's it", always in this order and with this wording:
   - P1: *"Husker du noe mer?"* / *"Do you remember anything else?"*
   - P2: *"Var det noe mer som skjedde, før eller etter det?"* / *"Did anything else happen, before or after that?"*
   - P3 (cued invitation, at most once, may only re-use the learner's own words): *"Du nevnte [X]. Fortell mer om det."* / *"You mentioned [X]. Tell me more about that."*
   Never name a character, place, object or event the learner has not already produced. Mark the timestamp of P1 in the transcript: everything before it is **free recall (F)**; everything after it is **prompted recall (P)**.
7. **Probe questions (Q).** 2–3 pre-written L1 questions, asked only after P is finished. Probes may name content; because they come last they cannot contaminate F or P. Score separately.
8. **Record per week.** wpm, F %, P %, F+P %, Q correct, D (distortions), I (intrusions), passage difficulty descriptors.

## Scoring rubric

Score each pre-listed idea unit once, using the first adequate mention; a unit first produced in P is credited to P even if elaborated later.

| Code | Rule | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| V/G | Verbatim or faithful paraphrase; pre-listed synonyms accepted; a minor inaccuracy that does not alter the idea (mangled proper name) is credited | 1 |
| PART | Core predicate correct but one required element missing or vague (e.g. agent omitted, "someone went") | 0.5 |
| DIST | Unit mentioned but meaning wrong: wrong agent or patient, missed or added negation, wrong pronoun referent, wrong time or causal link, wrong required detail | 0, and +1 to **D** |
| — | Not mentioned | 0 |

Separately count intrusions, **I**, for content not in the text: I-elab (plausible elaborative inference, Horiba) and I-false (contradicts the text or is invented). For every DIST and I-false, tag the most likely Bernhardt factor where the cause is evident from the Arabic: word recognition (wrong lexical sense), graphemic (visually similar word), syntax (misparsed agent/patient, negation, clause relation), intratextual (failed to link to earlier text), prior knowledge (imported from the learner's world knowledge). Leave the tag blank rather than guess.

Report: **F %** (free units ÷ total), **P %** (additional units after P1 ÷ total), **F+P %**, **main-idea share** (main units recalled ÷ main units), **D**, **I**, **Q**, **wpm**. Primary trend metrics **[inference]**: F+P % together with D, read alongside wpm (a rising wpm with stable F+P % and falling D is the pattern of growing fluency). Secondary: F % alone, the purest but noisiest index. Do not weight units by importance in the total (Heinz, Schmidt-Rinehart: weighting adds nothing), but do report the main-idea share, since Riley & Lee show recall otherwise over-represents details.
